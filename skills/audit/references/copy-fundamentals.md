# Copy fundamentals

The classic principles `copywriting` falls back on where the author's own voice says nothing. They come second in the order of authority: the author's voice first (a style guide, a voice note in the README or CLAUDE.md, or the copy they've already written and kept), then these, then any other general advice. Where the author's voice disagrees with a fundamental, the voice wins and the fundamental isn't raised.

Each section gives the idea in a sentence or two, then the **lenses**: concrete things to look for in real copy on a rendered page. A suggestion that rests on a fundamental names it, briefly, in the form `Krug: omit needless words` or `Schwartz: stages of awareness`, so the author can tell a borrowed rule from their own.

These are summaries in this repo's own words. Read the sources for the full argument.

## Eugene Schwartz: stages of awareness

Copy can't create desire; it can only meet the desire a reader already has and point it at the product. Where to start depends on how much the reader already knows. There are five stages:

- **Unaware.** They don't know they have the problem. Copy starts with them: a situation or identity they recognise, not the product.
- **Problem-aware.** They feel the problem but don't know a fix exists. Copy names the problem in their words, then shows it can be solved.
- **Solution-aware.** They know fixes exist but not this one. Copy shows why this one, against the alternatives they're weighing.
- **Product-aware.** They know this product but haven't acted. Copy removes the last doubts: proof, specifics, risk.
- **Most aware.** They're ready. Copy gets out of the way: the offer and the button.

Lenses: a home page pitching features to visitors who don't yet know they have the problem; a pricing or sign-up page still explaining what the product is; one page trying to speak to every stage at once, so the headline fits none.

Source: Eugene Schwartz, *Breakthrough Advertising*.

## David Ogilvy: the headline does the work

Far more people read the headline than the body, so the headline carries the promise. The reader is intelligent and busy: tell them facts, plainly, and don't be clever at their expense.

- **The benefit is in the headline.** Lenses: a headline that's a pun, a slogan or the company name, with no promise in it; a headline that only makes sense after reading the paragraph below.
- **Specific beats general.** Lenses: "fast", "powerful", "trusted by teams" where a number, a name or a fact would do ("loads in 0.8s", "used by 40 design teams").
- **Captions get read.** People read the line under an image more than the body around it. Lenses: images, screenshots and case study figures with no caption, or a caption that only labels ("Dashboard") instead of saying what to notice.
- **Long copy is fine if every line informs.** Lenses: copy cut so short it leaves the reader's real questions unanswered (price, who it's for, what happens next); length made of adjectives rather than facts.

Source: David Ogilvy, *Ogilvy on Advertising* and *Confessions of an Advertising Man*.

## Steve Krug: don't make me think

A page should be self-evident: a visitor should get it without effort, and every question it makes them ask costs attention.

- **Omit needless words.** Cut half the words on the page, then half of what's left. Lenses: a paragraph you could delete without losing anything; sentences that restate the heading above them.
- **Happy talk must die.** Lenses: welcome paragraphs ("We're so glad you're here…"), mission statements before any information, self-congratulation.
- **Instructions must die.** If a control needs explaining, the control is the problem. Lenses: a sentence next to a button or form telling people how to use it.
- **Plain names over clever ones.** Lenses: marketing-coined names for ordinary things (a "Workspace Hub" that's just settings), navigation labels people have to decode.

Source: Steve Krug, *Don't Make Me Think*.

## Nielsen Norman Group: people scan

On the web, people read a small fraction of the words on a page, mostly the start of headings, links and paragraphs, in a rough F shape down the left side. Write for that.

- **Front-load.** The words that matter come first in headings, links, list items and paragraphs. Lenses: headings that open with filler ("Introducing our…", "How we can help you with…"); the key word arriving at the end of a long line.
- **Inverted pyramid.** The conclusion first, then the detail. Lenses: a page that builds up to its point; the answer to "what is this?" below the fold.
- **One idea per paragraph.** Lenses: long paragraphs mixing several points; lists written as prose.
- **Link text says where it goes.** Lenses: "click here", "learn more", "read more" repeated down a page, each meaningless out of context.

Source: Nielsen Norman Group's research on how people read on the web (nngroup.com).

## George Orwell: six rules

General writing rules, and the base under everything above:

1. Don't use a figure of speech you're used to seeing in print.
2. Use the short word where a long one will do.
3. If a word can be cut, cut it.
4. Use the active voice where you can.
5. Use everyday words over jargon and borrowed phrases.
6. Break any of these rules sooner than write something clumsy.

Lenses: stock phrases ("in today's fast-paced world", "take it to the next level"); business jargon ("leverage", "seamless", "solutions", "empower", "unlock"); passive sentences that hide who does what ("mistakes were made", "your data is protected" with no word on how or by whom).

Source: George Orwell, "Politics and the English Language".

## April Dunford: positioning comes first

Copy can only be as clear as the positioning behind it. Positioning answers: what would the customer use if this didn't exist (the competitive alternatives), what this has that they don't (unique attributes), what that does for the customer (value), who cares most about that value (the target), and what category it belongs to so readers know how to judge it.

Lenses: a page that never says who it's for; claims every competitor could also make ("easy to use", "all-in-one"); features listed with no value attached; a category the reader can't name after the first screen; differences from the real alternative (often a spreadsheet, an agency or doing nothing) left unsaid.

Source: April Dunford, *Obviously Awesome*.

## Kathy Sierra: make them badass

People don't want to be good at your product; they want to be good at what the product lets them do. Copy that sells is about the reader's results, the "compelling context", not the tool.

- **Sell the result, not the tool.** Lenses: copy that describes the product's features ("advanced filters, custom tags") instead of what the reader can then do ("find any client's files in seconds"); a portfolio that lists the author's skills instead of what clients got.
- **Show the badass, not the product.** Lenses: testimonials that praise the product ("great tool!") rather than describe what the person achieved with it; screenshots of an empty interface instead of real work done in it.
- **Save the reader's attention.** Every confusing word, unanswered question or extra choice drains the attention they need to act. Lenses: too many choices at once (five calls to action on one screen); terms used before they're explained.

Source: Kathy Sierra, *Badass: Making Users Awesome*.
